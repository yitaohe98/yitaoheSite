After my first small website experiment, I wanted the next project to teach me more than how to ship another application. I wanted to practise finding demand, speaking with buyers, testing willingness to pay, and understanding whether a project could become a business.

I compared six options: a rental and utility record app, a stock-strategy execution tool, an offline card game, a friend's e-commerce business, digital materials, and a poker training product. The comparison made the decision clearer: the strongest project was the one with an existing buyer and an existing business.

## The useful question was not “What can I build?”

I already know that I can build websites, backend systems, and AI-assisted features. The harder question is whether anyone has a concrete reason to use and pay for what I build.

For each option, I looked at:

- Whether the need came from current behaviour rather than a casual suggestion
- Whether I could reach the user or buyer directly
- Whether someone had already spent money or time solving the problem
- Whether I could test willingness to pay before building heavily
- Whether the project could create reusable assets or future distribution
- How limited the downside would be if the hypothesis was wrong

This changed the ranking. Technical interest mattered, but evidence mattered more.

## The strongest evidence was in a friend's business

The e-commerce option is different from the other ideas because it is attached to an operating business. My friend has sold toys for about three years through established marketplaces, has existing customers and sales history, and earns a 6–7 figure annual profit.

He actively wants an independent website as another sales channel and is willing to pay for it. That does not prove the website will generate traffic. Marketplaces currently provide discovery, trust, payments, and transaction support. But it does prove that there is a real customer, a real budget, and a real commercial context.

The first delivery should be a defined website project, with clear boundaries around:

- Initial build and product-data setup
- Third-party services and platform fees
- Product photography, translations, and fulfilment
- Bug fixes versus new features
- Maintenance and ongoing support
- Traffic, advertising, and sales responsibilities

The main risk is not whether I can build the site. It is allowing a paid project to become indefinite free support.

## The deeper opportunity may be customer service

The website is the clearest first delivery, but the most painful problem may be customer service. The business owner needs very fast replies, sometimes within a minute. That affects sleep, working hours, labour costs, and the quality of customer communication.

The right first version is not a fully autonomous chatbot. It is an internal copilot that can:

1. Receive a customer question
2. Retrieve product and policy information
3. Suggest a reply
4. Let a human approve or edit it
5. Record corrections and unusual cases

Only reliable, repetitive questions should later be answered automatically. Before building too far, I need to confirm that the marketplace APIs expose the messages, orders, and logistics data the system would need.

If the workflow works, the product could grow from reply suggestions into a knowledge base, partial automation, order integration, and eventually a customer-service system for cross-border sellers. That is a promising product direction, but it should emerge from a real workflow rather than from a generic chatbot idea.

## Digital materials are the independent experiment

The second project I want to keep alive is digital materials: printable games, activity packs, educational cards, and personalised products sold through an existing marketplace.

This option has lower immediate cash flow but stronger ownership. A successful catalogue could accumulate reviews, keyword knowledge, production templates, customer history, and a future audience. The important caveat is that digital products are easy to copy and generic AI-generated artwork is highly commoditised.

I therefore do not want to produce hundreds of random files. The experiment should be narrow:

- Research ten micro-niches
- Score demand, competition, and personal advantage
- Choose two or three focused directions
- Launch a small, coherent collection
- Measure impressions, clicks, purchases, reviews, and customisation requests
- Expand only around demonstrated demand

Printable games, travel activity packs, educational resources, bilingual learning cards, and personalised products seem more promising than generic wall art. The asset I want to build is not just a folder of files. It is a catalogue, a niche, a repeatable production process, and knowledge about what buyers actually choose.

## What I am not choosing yet

The other options are useful, but the evidence is weaker or the risk is higher.

A rental and utility app has an excellent first user, but one user does not establish a repeatable market. A stronger trading-execution product has stated willingness to pay, but that willingness is connected to future investment returns and brings regulatory, security, and liability problems. The offline card game is a good low-risk launch experiment, though monetisation and distribution are uncertain.

I am also ruling out a bot that plays real-money poker. A legitimate poker training product could be interesting, but secretly automating play would violate platform rules and create a poor foundation for a business.

## The portfolio is intentionally unbalanced

The decision is not to split my time equally between two ideas.

The friend's business is the primary commitment because it offers immediate revenue, direct customer access, real operational problems, and reusable commercial experience. Digital materials remain a tightly controlled side experiment because they offer ownership and a way to practise independent market validation.

The combination gives me two different kinds of learning:

- A paid B2B project teaches scope, trust, delivery, maintenance, and distribution.
- A small catalogue experiment teaches demand research, marketplace positioning, conversion, and compounding assets.

The principle I want to carry forward is simple: choose the project with the strongest evidence of a buyer, then use the work to discover the next problem worth solving. Technical novelty can still matter, but it should not be the main reason I commit.
